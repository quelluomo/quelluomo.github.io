Voice and Tone Guide

This is the writing guide from Building a UX Writing System, as the team used it. I've removed the product name, the brand values and the real names in the examples, and tidied the wording. The rules and the examples are unchanged.

Why we need voice and tone guidelines

Our product exists to help teams give their customers great service. Writing in a consistent, natural voice is one of the ways we do that.

These guidelines make sure every piece of text sounds like it comes from the same place, whoever wrote it. They help us come across as what we are: passionate, smart and committed professionals who want to make our users' work easier.

Our voice

The essence of our voice

Our voice reflects our passion for solving problems and for good customer experiences. It is neutral yet slightly casual in form, and neutral but slightly enthusiastic in tone. It's smart, respectful and committed. The goal is to come across as knowledgeable and approachable.

Key attributes

  • Neutral: keep a balanced, professional manner. Avoid being either very formal or very casual.
  • Respectful: show consideration for a diverse audience and value their perspectives and needs.
  • Slightly casual in form: be friendly and approachable, so people find it easy to connect with us.
  • Slightly enthusiastic in tone: let our passion for the work show. Sound knowledgeable and easy to understand, so readers feel confident and engaged.

Writing guidelines

Tone

These scales help you write copy that fits our voice and tone. Each one leans to one side, but none of them is set in stone: adjust your copy when the context asks for it.

Clarity and simplicity

  • Use plain language: avoid jargon and technical terms that might confuse readers, especially agents and anyone without a strong technical background. With developers and specialists, be comprehensive and thorough.
  • Be concise: keep sentences and paragraphs short and to the point.
  • Focus on the reader: whenever possible, speak to the reader directly with "you". It makes the text personal and engaging.

Consistency

  • Use a consistent style: keep terminology, punctuation and formatting the same everywhere.
  • Stay in voice: every piece of writing should sound neutral, respectful and approachable, as described above.

Inclusivity

  • Use inclusive language: avoid terms that might exclude or alienate any group of people.
  • Be culturally aware: respect the diverse backgrounds of our audience.

Enthusiasm and respect

  • Show enthusiasm: let our passion for the work come through, without overwhelming the reader.
  • Maintain respect: always respect the reader's time, intelligence and perspective.

Make content easy to scan

  • Use short paragraphs and highlight keywords: readers skim. Highlighted words help them find what matters.
  • Break up walls of text: use subheadings and short paragraphs to make text easier to read.

Do and don't

Use simple language

Write clearly, with simple words. Jargon is fine when your readers have a technical background and you know the message will land. In most cases, simple and clear sentences work best.

  • Do: "Did you mean gmail.com?"
  • Don't: "Error details: email_channel_not_found"

Be concise

A sentence should express one idea. Aim for no more than 20 words per sentence, unless you really need more.

  • Do: "Press the add button to start"
  • Don't: "In order to get started the add button should be selected by the user by clicking on it"

Be inclusive

Not everyone shares the same background or experiences. Use language that works for people from all walks of life.

  • Do: "Show all"
  • Don't: "See all". Not everyone sees the screen, and words like "Show", "Go" or "Play" work for every reader.

Use the active voice

In the active voice, the subject does the action instead of having the action done to it. It's clearer, more dynamic and easier to read.

  • Do: "Alex Doe updated their profile last week"
  • Don't: "The profile has been updated by the user Alex Doe last week"

Be polite

Giving readers orders sounds harsh and intimidating. Guide them through the application instead.

  • Do: "Log in to continue"
  • Don't: "You must be logged in to continue"

Be to the point

Whenever possible, put the action the reader needs to take at the start of the sentence.

  • Do: "Save your progress before continuing"
  • Don't: "Before continuing, you need to save your progress"

Handling specific situations

Errors

Empathise with the reader. An error is a moment of frustration and confusion, and the message needs to guide them out of it.

  • Acknowledge the error: "Something went wrong."
  • Offer a solution: "Please try refreshing the page or contact support if the problem persists."
  • Stay calm and respectful: "We apologise for the inconvenience and are here to help."

Progress and completion

  • Celebrate milestones: "The dashboard has been created successfully."
  • Give the next step: "Click here to open it."

Encouragement

  • Motivate the reader: "You're doing great! Keep up the good work."
  • Offer support: "Need help? Our support team is just a click away."

Blocking errors and problems

  • Be clear and helpful: "We couldn't process your request because of missing information. Please check and try again."
  • Give guidance: "Here's what you can do to resolve the issue…"

New features

  • Highlight the benefits: "Discover our latest feature that makes managing your tasks even easier!"
  • Encourage exploration: "Try it out today and see how it can improve your workflow."

In short

Following these guidelines keeps everything we write clear, consistent and inclusive. It makes the product sound like one product, and it makes it easier to use for everyone who reads it.
