@ Nolan Bushnell, Atari founder and Co-President

My very first experience with electronic games, if you were to consider them as video games, was at the University of Utah. At the time there were five places in the whole world with video screens connected to a computer. The University of Utah happened to be one.

I mean, I can remember one arcade game that had nothing more than a rolling carpet with a little skier on a couple of rods and I mean, it was making big money, so... you know, I said “Hey, I can do better than that!”

Syzygy started in my daughter’s bedroom. I had to move my daughters together in the same room and use one as my office.

The first video game that I made was a game called *Computer Space,* which was loosely patterned after a game Steve Russell did on the PDP10 at the M.I.T., and hmmm, it was a flying saucer moving through rocks, and, you know, it was cool, all my friends loved it. All my friends were engineers.

We were… I considered Atari to be a little social experiment. It was very, very interesting.

It was the early 70s in California. There were lots of hippies around, and rock music, and we were very young. We were a company, but we didn’t act like one. We had parties and things like that. So we managed to get eye care, and prescriptions, and glasses for virtually nothing, because nobody needed it. But they made a huge mistake because they put in orthodontia and given all the teenagers working for us at the time in two weeks we had half of our staff queuing up to put braces on and get their teeth straightened!

People asked me ‘Did you ever think it was going to be this big?’ and it was difficult to quantify what ‘big’ was in those days. Yes, we were doing millions of dollars in sales and that sort of stuff, but in reality we didn’t have any other reference. Atari was able to attract the best of the brightest, and when you get in a room with some of those guys, and all the synapsis connect… there was this kind of electricity in the air. Going out to find the best of the brightest and being able to bring them in was incredibly intense. You know, at that time in the Silicon Valley everyone was going after the bare cubicle, with this humanly detached approach… and we didn’t put up an office. We had bathtubs and this sort of thing. I can remember driving in one morning into the parking lot, and we had this parking lot and it was completely full, with cars parked in two sides. I thought to myself ‘You know, games are indirectly paying for all these cars’. And somehow, the physical actuality of those cars and knowing how much they cost... and there were so many of ‘em... that was kind of... it was kind of a defining moment. I said ‘Wow! Wow, I created a monster here!’
